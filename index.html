1 · What we play

How Australians Stay Active: Activities, Ages, Organisers and Tech

Participation in sports and activities among Australians aged 15 and over

Key insights

  • Recreational walking is the most popular activity, with roughly 11 million Australians aged 15+ taking part, about 1.5 times as many as fitness/gym.

  • Fitness/gym, bushwalking, running/jogging and swimming follow, each with roughly 3.5–8 million participants, while other top-25 activities have under 2 million.

  • Team and field sports such as volleyball, handball, gridiron and Oztag draw most of their players from young people, with around 55–65% aged 15–24.

  • Low-intensity activities such as croquet and bowls are dominated by Australians aged 65+, while golf and walking also draw about a quarter from this age group.

Who Organises Australia's Sports and Activities?

The organiser behind an activity depends on what that activity needs. Competition runs through clubs, facilities and equipment through businesses, and first exposure through schools.

Key insights

  • Sports clubs run competitive sport. Clubs are the main organiser for team and traditional sports such as Hockey (69%), Shooting/field (69%), and Sailing (65%).
  • Commercial businesses lead lifestyle fitness and equipment-based activities. Pilates (61%), Yoga (54%) and Dancing (51%) are mostly delivered by paid providers. So are Underwater sports (62%), Canoeing/Kayaking (43%) and Ice skating (43%).
  • Gyms and leisure centres anchor strength and fitness training. Crossfit (47%) and Fitness/Gym (45%) are mainly organised through gyms.
  • Personal coaches are a major secondary organiser. They account for 22% of Fitness/Gym and 25% of Weight lifting mentions, and lead Equestrian (35%).
  • Everyday activities have no dominant organiser. Running, Walking, Cycling and Bush walking are spread across all organiser types, reflecting their largely informal, self-organised nature.

Note: Participants could name more than one organiser, so percentages show each organiser's share of mentions, not of participants. Activities with very few survey responses have been removed, and results for other small activities are less precise.

Technology and the Modern Athlete

Which technology people use during sport shifts with age, from entertainment for younger participants to health tracking for older ones.

Key insights

  • The same five technologies stay on top. Music apps, headphones, tracking apps, wearables and Maps/GPS are the top five in every age group; only their order changes.
  • Gaming-based exercise is mainly for teenagers. Motion-sensing games rank 8th among 15–17 year olds but fall to 14th among those aged 65+. Use drops from 11% to under 1%.
  • Bike computers rise from 12th to 6th with age. Their usage stays at 6–9% across all ages.
  • Few people pay for digital training. Online training platforms and paid online programs rank near the bottom for every age group.

Note: Ranks show how popular each technology is within an age group, not how many people use it. Overall technology use declines with age, e.g. music app use falls from 66% of 15–17 year olds to 12% of those aged 65+.

2 · Why we move

Why Australians Stay Active and Why They don't

Motivations and Barriers to Physical Activity in Australia

Why Do Australians Play Sport?

Australians share the same top reasons for playing sport: physical health and fun rank first and second across every age group and both genders. Beyond these, motivations shift with life stage.

Key insights

  • Health is the leading motivation, and it grows with age. Physical health or fitness motivates 62% of 15–17 year olds, rising to 74% of those aged 65+.
  • Young people play for fun, friends and challenge. 15–17 year olds are more likely than those aged 65+ to play for fun (72% vs 50%), social reasons (57% vs 40%) and competition (35% vs 9%).
  • Older adults exercise to stay mobile. Physio and rehabilitation motivates 16% of those aged 65+, nearly three times the 6% of 15–17 year olds.
  • Weight management and being outdoors peak before retirement. Both are highest among 55–64 year olds (60% and 58%). Weight management then falls to 46% at 65+.
  • Women are driven more by wellbeing. Women lead men on mental health (42% vs 33%), weight management (56% vs 51%), being outdoors (54% vs 48%) and walking the dog (24% vs 16%).
  • Men are driven more by performance. Men lead women on competition (18% vs 10%), training (25% vs 16%) and playing as a hobby (32% vs 25%).
  • Few play sport for work. Only 4% of adults are motivated by professional or work-related reasons, the lowest of all motivations.

What Stops Australians Being Active?

Reasons for not being active are more varied than reasons for playing. The most common barriers relate to physical condition (poor health, age and disability) and personal preference (low motivation, disliking sport and other priorities).

Key insights

  • Physical condition is the biggest barrier. Poor health or injury and increasing age are the most common reasons adults are inactive.
  • Barriers shift from attitude to the body with age.
  • Confidence holds young people back. 15–17 year olds are the most likely to feel not good enough (17.2%), have nobody to be active with (17.0%) or fear discrimination (10.0%).
  • Barriers are similar regardless of gender. Men and women report broadly the same reasons for not being active.
  • Access is rarely the issue. Only a small proportion cite a lack of facilities or transport. This suggests inactivity is driven by health and attitude rather than infrastructure.

3 · Over time

How Australians' Participation Has Changed Over Time

How has adult participation in physical activity changed between 2024 and 2025?

Participation Trends by Gender, 2016-2025

Women have consistently been as active as, or more active than, men, and participation for both genders rose between 2024 and 2025.

Why the line drops after 2022

The fall between 2022 and 2024 reflects a change in how the survey was run, not a real drop in activity:

  • The survey method changed. In July 2023, AusPlay moved from phone interviews to online surveys, and made other improvements at the same time.
  • The two methods measure participation differently. People can answer differently to an interviewer than when completing a form online, so the two sets of results sit at different levels.
  • The two periods can't be compared. The Australian Sports Commission treats 2024 onward as a new baseline that cannot be directly compared with 2016-2022. Trends should only be compared within each period.

Key insights

  • Women lead, especially in regular activity. From 2016 to 2022, women were more active than men every year. The gap was widest for activity three or more times a week, at around 3 percentage points.
  • About half of adults are active three times a week. In 2025, around 50% of adults were active at least three times weekly, compared with about 69% at least once a week.

Rising or Falling? Participation by Activity, 2024-2025

Between 2024 and 2025, participation in most activities barely changed. The biggest shifts were in Fitness/Gym and Pilates, while several traditional sports declined slightly.

Key insights

  • Fitness is driving growth. Fitness/Gym (+3.77 pp) and Pilates (+1.39 pp) recorded the largest gains of any activity, far ahead of all other sports.
  • Pickleball is the breakout sport. Pickleball/padel rose by +0.61 pp, the largest gain outside fitness and outdoor activities.
  • Some traditional sports are slipping, but not all. Cycling (−0.29 pp), basketball (−0.21 pp), athletics (−0.19 pp) and Australian football (−0.19 pp) recorded the largest falls outside the gym. Others, such as swimming (+0.20 pp), football/soccer (+0.19 pp) and cricket (+0.07 pp), rose slightly.
  • Skill- and location-dependent activities hold steady. Activities that take time to master or need specific locations, such as surfing, diving, sailing, equestrian and winter sports, changed by 0.02 pp or less. This suggests they rely on a small, committed base of participants, with few people taking them up or dropping them in a single year.
  • Most sports are stable. The majority of activities, including most team, water, combat, equestrian and winter sports, changed by less than 0.1 pp.

4 · Across the states

Mapping the Nation's Activity

Overall engagement, activity choice, and gender gaps between different demographic groups.

How Much Does Australia Move?

Physical activity participation is similar across Australian states, with most sitting within about 2 percentage points (pp) of the national rate.

Key insights

  • The Australian Capital Territory is Australia's most active jurisdiction. In 2025, 78.3% of adults in the Australian Capital Territory were active weekly, about 9.5 pp above the national rate of 68.8%.
  • The Northern Territory ranks second. The Northern Territory was around 4 pp above the national rate on both measures in 2025 (73.0% weekly, 54.4% three times weekly).
  • Western Australia has overtaken the national average in a year. Western Australia sat close to the national rate in 2024 but rose above it in 2025 (+2.1 pp weekly, +2.4 pp three times weekly). This indicates that participation in Western Australia grew faster than the national average over the year.
  • South Australia, New South Wales and Queensland trail the nation. All three were below the national rate in 2025. New South Wales had the lowest rate of regular activity, at 47.9% active three times a week, 2.2 pp below the national rate.

Where Is the Gender Gap Widest?

Across most of Australia, men and women are active at similar rates. The largest states stay within about 1 percentage point (pp) of parity. The widest gaps appear in the smaller states and territories.

Key insights

  • The largest states are close to equal. In New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland, the gap between women and men stayed within about 1 pp in both 2024 and 2025.
  • Tasmania has the most consistent gap. Women in Tasmania were more active than men in both years (+4.4 pp in 2024, +4.7 pp in 2025).
  • The Northern Territory shows the widest but least stable gap. It swung from men leading by 7.0 pp in 2024 to women leading by 10.8 pp in 2025.
  • Women lead more clearly in regular activity. For activity at least three times a week, women were more active than men in every state and territory except South Australia in 2025.

5 · Life & health

Life Shapes Movement

How a person's health and socioeconomic circumstances influence their activity levels.

Does Health Status Change How Active We Are?

Across every health factor, people in poorer health spend more time inactive and less time in vigorous activity. The healthiest group is the most active on almost every measure.

Key insights

  • A clear health gradient. For every health factor, activity falls from the lowest-risk to the highest-risk group.
  • Body size shows one of the largest gaps. Adults with obesity spend 12 hours 24 minutes a day inactive, about 1 hour more than those of a healthy weight (11 hours 23 minutes). They do a third as much vigorous activity (2 vs 6 minutes). Waist circumference shows the same pattern (12 hours 14 minutes vs 11 hours 21 minutes inactive). Low activity can contribute to weight gain, as fewer calories are burned each day.
  • Heart disease is linked to the lowest activity. Adults with heart, stroke or vascular disease spend 12 hours 51 minutes a day inactive, over 1 hour more than those with no condition (11 hours 47 minutes). They record the lowest light and moderate activity of any group, and just 1 minute of vigorous activity. This is expected, as intense exercise puts extra strain on the heart, and many people with these conditions are advised to limit strenuous activity.
